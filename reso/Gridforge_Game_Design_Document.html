GRID
FORGE

Build the grid. Power the people. Shape the future.

A 3D electricity-network strategy and management simulation about building a reliable, affordable and increasingly sustainable power system for a growing region.

Genre · Strategy / Simulation3D · Isometric / Free-camera presentationSingle-player foundationEducational by designMaster blueprint

1. Executive overview

Gridforge puts the player in charge of an electricity utility serving a region that grows from a small rural settlement into a complex, modern city. The player plans infrastructure, builds a functioning network, secures generation, routes electricity, sets tariffs, maintains assets, responds to incidents and earns the trust needed to expand.

The fantasy

Be the person who turns a dark, disconnected region into a thriving, powered community.

The central hook

The world lights up only when power is physically delivered through a working network.

The strategic tension

Reliability, affordability, financial health and sustainability compete for limited resources.

High concept

Gridforge is a systemic management game. Every pole, cable, transformer, generator, contract, tariff and maintenance decision contributes to a living electricity network. The player is not simply placing decorative power lines: they are operating a connected system with capacity, losses, demand, costs, asset condition and consequences.

2. Vision, pillars & design principles

Vision statement

Make the complexity of electricity infrastructure understandable, tangible and emotionally rewarding through a beautiful, readable simulation where every decision changes the region.

Core pillars

1 · Build a real network

Players plan topology, connect assets and customers, manage capacity and expand coverage.

2 · Keep the lights on

Generation and demand must balance. Failures have causes, affected areas and repair paths.

3 · Run a sustainable business

Revenue, operating costs, fuel, procurement, debt, maintenance and tariff choices matter.

4 · Earn public trust

Customers need reliable and affordable service, transparent communication and fair treatment.

5 · Evolve the energy mix

Move from dispatchable fossil generation toward renewables, storage and smarter demand management.

6 · Learn through action

Concepts are taught at the moment they become useful, using clear visual feedback and decisions.

Design principles

  • Legibility before complexity: show why a building is dark, why a line is overloaded and why a bill changed.
  • Cause and effect: every failure and penalty should have an understandable explanation.
  • Player agency: recommendations advise; they never silently make strategic decisions.
  • Fair challenge: incidents are telegraphed where possible and can be mitigated through preparation.
  • Visible progress: lighting, traffic, services and activity make network improvements tangible.
  • Meaningful trade-offs: no single upgrade or energy source solves every problem.
  • Respect player time: no artificial daily-login pressure, manipulative scarcity or punitive waiting.

3. Audience & positioning

Primary audience

  • Fans of city-building, tycoon, logistics and management games.
  • Players who enjoy optimization, planning, networks and interconnected systems.
  • Curious players interested in energy, climate, infrastructure and how cities function.

Secondary audience

  • Educators and learners exploring electricity systems and sustainability.
  • Simulation players who enjoy scenario challenges and sandbox experimentation.

Comparable experience references

Use broad genre references such as city builders, transport/logistics simulators and business-management games for pacing and clarity. Gridforge should differentiate itself through its physically connected electricity network, power-delivery visualization, utility economics and transition-to-clean-energy campaign.

4. Intended player experience

Emotional arc

Curiosity→First connection→First lights→Responsibility→Disruption→Recovery→Mastery

The player begins with a manageable problem: a few customers need power. The first successful connection should feel magical. Complexity then grows gradually: more customers, higher demand, capacity constraints, money, maintenance, weather and policy. The player should feel capable of understanding the system even when the challenge becomes demanding.

Key moments to design for

  • First generator starts and the town receives electricity.
  • A pulse travels along a completed network path.
  • A clinic or school becomes powered for the first time.
  • A warning reveals that demand is approaching a capacity limit.
  • A fault causes a visible outage with a clear cause.
  • The player repairs the fault and watches the affected district relight.
  • A renewable project and storage system reduce fossil dependence.
  • The final city achieves a stable, affordable, high-renewable electricity system.

5. Core gameplay loop

Moment-to-moment loop

Observe→Diagnose→Plan→Build / dispatch→Simulate→Evaluate→Improve

Full strategic loop

  1. Assess the region: inspect customer locations, demand, existing assets, terrain, constraints and objectives.
  2. Plan infrastructure: choose generation, routes, poles, cables, transformers and substations.
  3. Connect customers: ensure every customer has a valid path through the network.
  4. Secure electricity: dispatch owned generation, buy from suppliers or use storage.
  5. Balance the system: keep generation and imports sufficient for demand while respecting line and transformer capacities.
  6. Serve customers: deliver energy over time; track kWh supplied and service quality.
  7. Collect revenue: bill customers based on energy consumed, tariff and customer class.
  8. Operate and maintain: monitor asset condition, fuel, inventory, contracts and operating costs.
  9. Respond to events: diagnose faults, restore service, communicate and mitigate future risks.
  10. Expand and transition: reinvest, unlock technologies, add capacity and improve environmental performance.

Core loop success condition

Each mission defines a measurable outcome: number of customers served, reliability target, affordability limit, budget, emissions target, renewable share or a combination. The player should be able to inspect progress and understand exactly what remains.

6. World structure & campaign

The campaign is a sequence of increasingly complex regions. Each stage introduces a new strategic layer while reusing familiar systems. The player should not be confronted with every system at once.

Campaign overview

StageRegionNew systemsCore challengeMilestone
1. The BeginningSmall rural townBasic generator, poles, low-voltage cable, transformer, homes and public servicesCreate a valid network and deliver enough powerFirst reliable powered settlement
2. Growing DemandDeveloping town / cityPopulation growth, customer classes, tariffs, bills, cash flow, peak demand, basic failuresExpand without pricing customers out or exhausting financesAffordable, reliable urban service
3. Industrial RevolutionIndustrial corridorHigh-voltage transmission, substations, large loads, congestion, industrial contracts, advanced maintenanceMove bulk power efficiently and meet demanding contractsStable industrial grid
4. The Green TransitionCoastal / mixed regionSolar, wind, hydro/tidal options, battery storage, prosumers, exports, weather variabilityManage intermittent supply and severe weather while decarbonizingHigh renewable share with reliable service
5. The 100% Green CityLarge metropolitan regionSmart grid, EV charging, demand response, advanced storage, efficiency, distributed energyMeet all consumption with clean electricity across a complex systemReliable, affordable 100% green electricity consumption

Stage structure

  • Opening briefing and visual introduction to the region.
  • Primary objective and one or two optional objectives.
  • Guided first use of new mechanic(s).
  • Free-play execution window with contextual advice.
  • Mid-mission complication or decision.
  • Performance evaluation and rewards.
  • Short debrief explaining what the player learned and what unlocks next.

Mission types

  • Build: create a network under budget and material constraints.
  • Restore: recover a district after a fault or disaster.
  • Optimize: reduce losses, costs or emissions without lowering service quality.
  • Resilience: prepare for and withstand a forecast event.
  • Affordability: improve service while meeting tariff and household affordability constraints.
  • Transition: replace or supplement fossil generation with clean energy and storage.

Sandbox and replayability (post-core)

Future modes may include open-ended sandbox regions, scenario editor, challenge missions, difficulty modifiers and seeded maps. These are expansion features, not prerequisites for the first playable vertical slice.

7. Simulation systems

7.1 Demand system

Each customer has a connection state, nominal demand, time-of-day demand profile, priority class, satisfaction and energy consumption. Demand can vary by hour, season, weather, population and business activity.

Customer classDemand behaviourPriority / sensitivity
Low-income householdSmall baseline; affordability-sensitiveHigh social sensitivity
Regular householdMorning/evening peaksReliability and price sensitive
CommercialDaytime-heavy; business-hour patternsOutage costs affect satisfaction and economy
IndustrialLarge, contract-based and sometimes continuousHigh capacity and reliability requirements
Public institutionSchool, clinic, water and emergency servicesHigh service priority
ProsumerConsumes and can generate/export surplusRequires metering and settlement rules

7.2 Generation system

Generation assets have maximum output, ramp behaviour, availability, fuel or resource input, operating cost, emissions, maintenance state and connection requirements. Dispatchable sources can be scheduled; variable renewables depend on weather or resource conditions.

SourceStrengthConstraint / trade-off
DieselFast to deploy, dispatchable, useful for early game and backupFuel cost, emissions, supply-chain exposure
CoalLarge steady outputHigh emissions, fuel logistics, inflexible operation
GasDispatchable and flexibleFuel price and supply risk, emissions
SolarLow operating cost and clean generationDaylight and weather variability; land/roof constraints
WindClean generation with strong resource potentialVariable wind; siting and maintenance
HydroPotentially controllable and low-carbonGeography, water availability and ecological trade-offs
TidalPredictable renewable resource in suitable locationsSpecific geography, infrastructure cost and ecological constraints

7.3 Transmission and distribution

  • Network topology is a graph of nodes and edges: sources, substations, transformers, junctions and customers.
  • Each line has voltage tier, capacity, length, condition, resistance/loss characteristics and construction cost.
  • Each transformer has input/output voltage, capacity, efficiency, condition and failure probability.
  • Connections must be physically and electrically valid; unsupported voltage connections are rejected with an explanation.
  • Overloaded assets can trigger warnings, increased losses, accelerated wear or protective trips depending on severity and duration.

7.4 Storage

Battery systems have power rating (kW/MW), energy capacity (kWh/MWh), state of charge, charge/discharge efficiency, degradation and operating limits. Storage can absorb surplus generation, cover peaks, provide backup and reduce curtailment; it cannot create energy.

7.5 Network losses

Energy is lost during transmission and distribution. A simplified model may calculate losses from line length, current/load, voltage tier and asset condition. The player sees energy generated/imported, energy delivered, and the difference attributed to network losses.

7.6 Reliability and asset health

Assets accumulate wear through age, loading, weather and incidents. Maintenance improves condition and reduces failure risk. Preventive maintenance costs money and may require temporary service interruption; reactive repair is more urgent and often more expensive.

7.7 Customer satisfaction and trust

Customer trust responds to outage frequency/duration, affordability, service quality, fairness, communication and restoration performance. Trust changes gradually and should be explainable through a breakdown panel rather than a hidden arbitrary score.

7.8 AI Grid Advisor

The advisor provides contextual hints, alerts, explanations and optional strategic recommendations. It can point out a bottleneck, forecast demand or compare options, but the player chooses whether to act. The advisor should distinguish facts, estimates and recommendations.

8. Economy, finance & stakeholders

8.1 Revenue streams

  • Retail electricity sales to households, businesses and public institutions.
  • Industrial supply contracts and connection fees.
  • Export revenue from prosumer surplus or interregional trading where enabled.
  • Government grants, resilience funding and clean-energy incentives.
  • Ancillary services or grid-support payments in advanced stages.

8.2 Cost categories

  • Generation operating costs and fuel purchases.
  • Electricity procurement from external suppliers or neighbouring regions.
  • Infrastructure construction, upgrades and replacement.
  • Preventive maintenance, emergency repair and spare parts.
  • Staff, administration, financing costs and debt service.
  • Outage compensation, penalties, environmental compliance and customer support.

8.3 Tariff design

The player sets tariffs within regulatory rules. Tariffs must cover the sustainable cost of service over time while remaining affordable. A low tariff may improve sentiment but create losses; a high tariff may improve revenue but trigger affordability pressure, complaints, regulatory intervention or demand reduction.

Affordability constraint: if the cost to serve customers exceeds the affordable or regulated tariff ceiling, the solution should not be an unlimited price increase. The player must improve efficiency, reduce losses, negotiate procurement, change the energy mix, secure support or redesign service delivery.

8.5 Stakeholders

8.6 Prosumers and net metering

Customers with rooftop solar or other distributed generation consume their own production first where configured, then may export surplus. The player must manage bidirectional metering, export compensation, local voltage/capacity constraints and settlement. Prosumers can reduce peak demand or create reverse-flow challenges depending on network design.

8.7 Inventory and procurement

Construction and repair use finite inventory: poles, cable lengths, transformers, generators, panels, batteries, repair kits and specialist components. Inventory is replenished through procurement with cost and delivery lead time. The player chooses how much to reserve for emergencies versus spend on expansion.

9. Electrical grid model & simulation rules

9.1 Units and concepts

ConceptDefinition in gameExample
PowerInstantaneous rate of electricity use or productionkW or MW
EnergyPower accumulated over timekWh or MWh
DemandPower customers are requesting at this moment42 kW now
Peak demandHighest demand during a defined periodEvening peak of 60 kW
CapacityMaximum safe power an asset can carry or produce100 kW transformer
Load factorAverage demand divided by peak demand over a period0.65
LossesEnergy dissipated in network delivery5% illustrative loss

9.2 Power delivery validation

A customer is powered only if all required conditions are true:

  1. There is at least one active generation source, valid import contract or discharging storage source.
  2. A valid path exists from that source to the customer through compatible infrastructure.
  3. All intermediate transformers and switchgear are active and correctly configured.
  4. No required line, transformer or source is tripped or out of service.
  5. Available capacity is sufficient for the delivered load, or a defined controlled-shedding rule applies.
  6. Supply is available after network losses and any reserve/operating constraints.

9.3 Suggested simulation sequence

  1. Calculate customer demand profiles for the current simulation interval.
  2. Calculate available output from each generator, supplier contract and storage asset.
  3. Dispatch sources according to player priority, marginal cost, emissions policy or chosen operating mode.
  4. Route power through the network and evaluate voltage compatibility and capacity constraints.
  5. Calculate losses and delivered energy at customer meters.
  6. Apply protection logic for overloads, faults and unsafe conditions.
  7. Update asset wear, fuel, storage state of charge and outage timers.
  8. Calculate billing, revenue, operating costs, emissions and stakeholder metrics.
  9. Update alerts, objective progress and visual lighting state.

9.4 Load shedding and priority

When available supply is insufficient, the game should show a deficit and let the player choose an operating policy: equal rotating reduction, priority protection for critical services, contract-based curtailment or manual load shedding. Critical facilities should not be invulnerable; their protection should be a strategic choice with costs and consequences.

9.5 Simplification policy

Gridforge is a strategy game, not a professional power-system engineering tool. Early stages should use simplified, consistent rules and clear visual explanations. Advanced concepts can be layered in later, but the game must not present misleading cause-and-effect. Where the simulation abstracts a real-world process, label it as a simplified model in the learning panel.

Fairness rules

  • Major events should be forecast or signalled where feasible.
  • Severity should scale with stage, difficulty and player preparedness.
  • Every incident should expose affected assets, customers, estimated impact and available responses.
  • Failure should create a recovery challenge, not an unexplained instant loss.
  • Repeated failure should be traceable to a root cause such as overload, poor condition or lack of redundancy.

Outage response flow

Alert→Locate fault→Isolate→Repair / reroute→Restore→Review

11. Progression, unlocks & rewards

Progression layers

  • Campaign progression: complete mission objectives to unlock the next region.
  • Technology progression: research and unlock more capable or efficient assets.
  • Operational progression: improve staff, maintenance, forecasting and response capabilities.
  • Network progression: unlock voltage tiers, substations, automation and smart-grid tools.
  • Reputation progression: earn confidence from communities, regulators and financiers.

Research categories

Research treeExample unlocks
GenerationEfficient generators, advanced solar, wind forecasting, hydro controls
NetworkHigher-capacity cable, improved transformers, high-voltage lines, automation
StorageBattery chemistry, grid-scale storage, smart dispatch
OperationsPredictive maintenance, faster fault location, crew coordination
Customer & marketSmart meters, time-of-use tariffs, demand response, prosumer settlement
ResilienceFlood protection, storm hardening, cybersecurity and backup systems

Reward philosophy

Rewards should reinforce competence: new tools, expanded territory, better contracts, reduced costs, improved reliability or a new strategic option. Avoid rewards that are disconnected from the player's decisions.

12. Learning design & educational content

Gridforge teaches by letting players encounter a problem, try a solution and see a consequence. Avoid a large upfront rule board. Introduce only the information needed for the current decision.

Learning conceptWhen introducedHow the game teaches it
Generation, transmission, distributionStage 1Build a source-to-customer route and trace the power path.
Power vs energyStage 1–2Compare instantaneous kW demand with kWh delivered over a billing period.
Capacity and overloadStage 1–2Overload an undersized asset in a safe guided example; upgrade or split load.
Peak demandStage 2See evening demand rise above daytime average.
Tariffs and affordabilityStage 2Compare revenue, operating costs and household bill impact.
Grid lossesStage 2–3Compare source energy with metered customer energy and test upgrades.
High-voltage transmissionStage 3Move bulk power over distance using substations and voltage conversion.
Renewable intermittencyStage 4Watch solar/wind output change with weather and time.
StorageStage 4Charge during surplus and discharge during a peak or outage.
Prosumer / net meteringStage 4Customers consume their own generation and export surplus under a tariff.
Demand response and efficiencyStage 5Shift or reduce demand to avoid expensive peaks and new infrastructure.
Resilience and redundancyAcross campaignCompare a single-path network with alternative routes and backup supply.

Learning panel template

  1. What happened? State the observed event in plain language.
  2. Why it happened: Identify the system relationship that caused it.
  3. What can I do? Present two or three viable actions and their trade-offs.
  4. Real-world connection: One short explanation or optional deeper reading.

Accuracy and tone

Use plain language first, correct terminology second. Avoid pretending the simplified simulation is a full engineering model. Definitions should be short, optional and linked to the player's current task.

13. Narrative, worldbuilding & characters

Narrative premise

The player takes responsibility for a regional utility at a moment of opportunity. The community needs dependable electricity to grow, but resources are limited and expectations are high. Across the campaign, the player earns the ability to serve larger populations and shape the region's energy future.

Recurring characters

CharacterRoleVoice and function
Grid AdvisorAI operations assistantCalm, concise, evidence-led. Explains systems, surfaces risks and suggests options without taking control.
Maya RahmanCommunity representativeHuman impact, affordability, local priorities and public trust.
Daniel CarterFinancial advisorCash flow, debt, investment, procurement and long-term sustainability.
Engineer SofiaChief grid engineerNetwork safety, capacity, technical diagnosis, maintenance and restoration.
Minister ElenaGovernment / regulator representativePublic targets, compliance, affordability, resilience and environmental commitments.

Dialogue principles

  • Short, natural and purposeful; avoid long exposition.
  • Characters should disagree for understandable reasons, not become caricatures.
  • Dialogue reflects the player's actual choices and current system state where practical.
  • Critical information must also be available in UI; no essential instruction should be dialogue-only.

Opening sequence concept

  1. Fade in from darkness over a quiet rural settlement.
  2. Show unlit homes, a clinic, school and market; subtle ambient sound.
  3. Introduce the player as the new utility operator.
  4. Grid Advisor identifies the first generator and the settlement's unmet demand.
  5. Transition directly into the playable map with one clear first objective.

Ending concept

The final cinematic should reflect the player's performance. A successful ending shows a thriving region, dependable services, clean generation and an informed community. The narration should acknowledge trade-offs and the decisions that made the outcome possible.

Ending variants

  • Sustainable Grid: high reliability, affordability, trust and clean-energy performance.
  • Reliable Grid: strong service and financial stability, but slower transition.
  • Green but Fragile: high renewable share but insufficient resilience or storage.
  • Financial Crisis: infrastructure exists but costs, debt or procurement are unsustainable.
  • Public Trust Crisis: power is available, but affordability, fairness or communication failed.

  • Never rely on colour alone: pair colours with icons, labels, patterns or status text.

15. Art direction & visual language

Visual identity

Stylized low-poly 3D miniature world with readable silhouettes, warm inhabited spaces, cool ambient shadows and luminous energy paths. The environment should feel tactile and handcrafted rather than photorealistic or sterile.

World palette

Deep navy, slate blue, muted terrain, cool atmospheric haze.

Power palette

Electric cyan / blue for flow; amber for caution; red for faults; green for clean generation and healthy status.

Human warmth

Warm yellow windows, street lights, vehicle lights and public spaces when powered.

World composition

  • Readable road hierarchy, plots, homes, civic buildings, commercial areas and industrial zones.
  • Infrastructure should be visible at useful zoom levels without cluttering the town.
  • Poles, cables, transformers and substations need distinctive silhouettes.
  • Buildings should communicate customer type and priority through shape and contextual props.
  • Terrain and weather should affect planning but never obscure critical information.

Power visualization

  • Animated pulses travel from generation through connected assets toward customers.
  • Powered buildings gradually illuminate; unpowered buildings remain visibly dark.
  • Partial or unstable service can use flicker or status indicators, never ambiguous random flashing.
  • Overloaded lines show heat/glow or warning markers; faults show a clear break or hazard indicator.
  • Overlay modes should be legible and switchable without changing the underlying simulation.

Animation requirements

  • Generator start-up: mechanical movement, status transition, subtle exhaust/heat cue where appropriate.
  • Construction: placement ghost, assembly animation and completion effect.
  • Power-on: a traveling energy pulse followed by building lights switching on in sequence.
  • Failure: protective trip, local lights going out and an alert pulse.
  • Repair: crew/tool animation, asset status restoration and power returning.
  • Renewables: visible rotor motion, solar response to daylight and battery charge/discharge indicators.

Camera and readability

Default camera should support an isometric overview. Zoom levels should transition between regional planning, district layout and asset inspection. Camera motion must be smooth, bounded and never disorienting. A north indicator and optional reset-camera control should be available.

16. Audio, music & feedback

Audio identity

Ambient, calm and optimistic, with a subtle technological character. Music should support concentration rather than compete with decision-making. The soundscape evolves as the settlement becomes powered.

Sound categories

  • Environment: wind, distant town ambience, birds, traffic and weather.
  • Infrastructure: generator hum, transformer buzz, switch clicks, construction, repair tools and substation ambience.
  • Power feedback: soft electrical pulse, successful connection chime, grid activation swell.
  • Alerts: distinct but non-alarming sounds for warning, critical fault and objective completion.
  • UI: light clicks, selection, confirm, cancel and error cues.
  • Music: restrained adaptive layers that grow warmer and fuller as the region develops.

Audio accessibility

  • Independent music and effects volume controls.
  • Master mute / sound on-off setting.
  • All important audio alerts duplicated visually and, where useful, through text.
  • No essential gameplay information communicated by sound alone.

17. Accessibility & localization

  • Scalable UI and readable type at common desktop resolutions.
  • Colour-blind-safe status encoding with labels, icons and patterns.
  • Adjustable text size and contrast options where feasible.
  • Remappable or configurable controls in later production stages.
  • Pause at any time in single-player.
  • Subtitles for all voiced dialogue and meaningful audio cues.
  • Plain-language explanations and optional glossary.
  • Reduce motion option for pulses, camera effects and screen transitions.
  • Localization-ready text keys; avoid embedding essential text in artwork.

18. Technical blueprint

18.1 Architecture principles

  • Separate simulation state from presentation and UI.
  • Use deterministic simulation steps where practical for reproducible saves, tests and balancing.
  • Represent the electrical network as a graph with typed nodes and edges.
  • Use data-driven definitions for assets, customers, missions, events and research.
  • Make UI read from the simulation model; UI must not invent or own gameplay truth.
  • Support autosave and explicit save/load for the full game release.

18.2 Suggested major modules

18.3 Data model examples

Every asset definition should include a stable ID, display name, category, cost, construction time, required materials, capacity, efficiency, condition, operating cost, maintenance interval, failure profile and unlock requirement. Customer definitions should include type, demand profile, priority, tariff class, satisfaction sensitivity and connection requirements.

18.4 Performance and scale

  • Use level-of-detail and simplified visuals at regional zoom.
  • Update simulation at a controlled tick rate separate from render frame rate.
  • Aggregate distant customer visualization where individual detail is not needed.
  • Use event-driven UI updates for large lists and panels.
  • Profile pathfinding, network flow and event evaluation as map size grows.

18.5 Offline / first-playable implementation constraint

For a reviewable prototype that must run offline, deliver a self-contained HTML file with embedded CSS and JavaScript, generated art/audio and no external network dependencies. A canvas or WebGL implementation can create an isometric/3D-like presentation; if the build is 2.5D rather than true 3D, describe that honestly. The full production game may use a dedicated 3D engine and modular project structure.

18.6 Save data

Save the mission ID, world seed, simulation time, assets and connections, resource inventory, financial state, customer state, event state, research, objectives and settings. Validate save version and support migration for future updates.

  • Terrain tiles, roads, sidewalks, water, bridges, vegetation and street furniture.

Content production rule

Prioritize reusable modular assets and consistent visual language. Build a small number of polished, readable assets before expanding the catalogue. Each new asset must have a gameplay reason, not merely visual variety.

Balancing targets

  • The first successful power delivery should happen quickly after the player understands construction.
  • The first mission should be completable without requiring perfect optimization.
  • There should be enough resources to make at least one reasonable layout, but not unlimited freedom.
  • A basic failure should be recoverable with the provided repair resources.
  • Financial pressure should be introduced only after the player understands service delivery.
  • Later missions should require trade-offs rather than simply more construction.

Difficulty dimensions

Adjust map size, demand growth, resource availability, event frequency, repair costs, tariff constraints, forecast quality, financial buffer and objective strictness. Difficulty should not secretly alter electrical rules.

21. QA, telemetry & acceptance criteria

Core functional acceptance

  • Intro sequence is skippable and transitions correctly to the main menu.
  • Main menu includes Start / New Game, How to Play and Settings.
  • Controls are documented in How to Play, not crowded into the main menu.
  • Settings include sound on/off; review builds may include a direct ending-scene shortcut.
  • Escape pauses and resumes; simulation time and gameplay systems stop while paused.
  • Pause menu includes Resume, sound toggle, Restart Level and Back to Main Menu.
  • Fail screen includes Retry and Back to Main Menu.
  • Ending sequence is distinct and returns to the main menu.
  • Buildings only illuminate when power is validly delivered.
  • Disconnected, overloaded, tripped or unsupplied customers remain unpowered.
  • Construction validates cost, inventory, placement and connectivity rules.
  • Every major alert explains cause, impact and a possible response.
  • Victory and failure states are reachable through actual gameplay, not only menu shortcuts.
  • Audio can be muted and all essential audio information has a visual equivalent.
  • Restart resets the mission state and resources consistently.

Playtest questions

  • Can a new player explain why a building is not powered?
  • Can they identify the next useful action without external help?
  • Do they understand the difference between kW and kWh after the first mission?
  • Does the first lighting moment feel rewarding?
  • Does a failure feel fair and recoverable?
  • Can players see the financial consequence of a decision?
  • Are overlays and asset states readable at all supported zoom levels?
  • Does the player feel responsible for the whole system rather than simply completing chores?

Suggested telemetry for later builds

Track tutorial completion, time to first powered customer, construction errors, unpowered-customer duration, overload events, outage duration, repair choices, objective completion, tariff changes, resource shortages and player exits. Use telemetry to find confusion and balance issues, not to manipulate engagement.

First playable scope boundary

Build this first: one complete rural-town mission that demonstrates assessment, construction, customer connection, generation, supply/demand, basic revenue, maintenance, a recoverable fault, objective completion, victory and a separate ending scene. Do not attempt the entire five-stage economy and technology tree in the first implementation.

Out of scope for the first playable

  • Full five-stage campaign and all generation technologies.
  • Advanced electricity market simulation and complex bidding.
  • Full regulatory, political and investor negotiation systems.
  • Deep cybersecurity simulation, multiplayer and user-generated maps.
  • Professional-grade load-flow, voltage stability or protection engineering simulation.

23. Risks & mitigations

RiskWhy it mattersMitigation
System complexity overwhelms playersToo many interacting mechanics can feel opaque.Progressive onboarding, clear cause/effect panels, staged unlocks and optional advanced detail.
Grid model feels unrealistic or inconsistentPlayers may lose trust in the simulation.Use consistent rules, validate calculations and label abstractions honestly.
Network construction becomes tediousRepeated placement can dominate strategy.Smart snapping, route tools, copy/upgrade tools and clear placement previews.
Visual effects obscure statePower pulses and lights can become visual noise.Control effect intensity, provide overlays and reduce-motion settings.
Economy becomes punitivePlayers may fail before understanding why.Early safety net, transparent forecasts and recoverable first mistakes.
Scope expansion delays qualityFive stages and many systems are substantial.Prove one vertical slice before broad content production.
Performance drops on large mapsSimulation and rendering costs grow with network size.Profile early, use LOD, aggregation and controlled simulation ticks.
Educational content feels like homeworkPlayers disengage from long explanations.Teach through decisions, visual feedback and optional concise learning panels.

24. Glossary

TermPlain-language definition
GenerationProducing electricity from a source such as fuel, sunlight, wind or water.
TransmissionMoving large amounts of electricity over longer distances, typically at higher voltage.
DistributionDelivering electricity from substations through local networks to customers.
TransformerEquipment that changes voltage between parts of the electricity network.
SubstationA facility that switches, transforms and routes electricity between network levels.
kWKilowatt, a unit of power (rate of energy use or production).
kWhKilowatt-hour, a unit of energy delivered or consumed over time.
Peak demandThe highest level of electricity demand during a period.
Load sheddingIntentionally reducing or disconnecting some demand when supply is insufficient.
Grid lossEnergy lost while electricity travels through the network.
ProsumerA customer who both consumes and produces electricity.
Net meteringA billing arrangement that credits eligible electricity exported by a customer.

25. Appendix — First playable mission blueprint

Mission title: Light the First District

Setting: a small rural settlement at dusk. A single generator exists near the edge of town. Ten homes, a school, a clinic and a market need electricity. The player has a limited construction budget and must build a functioning distribution network.

Mission objectives

  1. Inspect the generator and identify total customer demand.
  2. Build a valid distribution route with poles and cables.
  3. Place and connect a transformer where required.
  4. Connect the school, clinic, market and homes.
  5. Start the generator and supply at least the required demand.
  6. Maintain a stable supply while earning initial revenue.
  7. Respond to a scripted transformer fault and restore affected customers.
  8. Finish with all required customers powered and the network stable.

Suggested scripted sequence

BeatWhat happensTeaching / feedback
OpeningTown is dark; camera reveals the settlement.Establish the emotional goal.
InspectPlayer selects generator and customer buildings.Show output, capacity and demand.
BuildPlayer places poles, cable and transformer.Placement preview and connection validation.
ConnectPlayer completes customer connections.Show valid source-to-customer path.
GeneratePlayer starts generator.Fuel consumption and available output appear.
First lightEnergy pulse travels through network; buildings illuminate.Major positive feedback moment.
OperateSimulation advances; customers consume energy and revenue accrues.Introduce kW vs kWh in context.
FaultOne transformer trips due to a scripted fault.Affected customers go dark; alert identifies the asset.
RepairPlayer inspects and repairs the transformer.Show repair cost, condition and restoration.
CompleteAll required customers remain powered for the target period.Victory summary and performance metrics.
EndingTown at night is fully lit; a short closing sequence plays.Return to main menu after the ending.

Victory metrics

  • Customers connected and powered.
  • Energy delivered (kWh).
  • Average and peak demand (kW).
  • Outage duration and restoration time.
  • Network losses (simplified percentage).
  • Credits earned and spent.
  • Asset condition at completion.

Failure conditions

  • Budget reaches zero before the minimum required network can be completed, if no recovery option remains.
  • Generator fuel is exhausted and no supply alternative exists for a defined period.
  • Critical service remains unpowered beyond the mission's allowed outage window.
  • Mission-specific stability requirement is not met before the deadline.

Failure handling

Do not fail the player for a single understandable mistake. Before failure, provide warnings and recovery options. The fail screen should explain the primary cause, show the relevant metrics and offer Retry or Main Menu. A first-time player should be able to retry immediately.

Minimum definition of done

  • One complete playable mission from title screen through ending.
  • Working selection, construction, connection and camera controls.
  • Functional power-source-to-customer validation.
  • Visible generation, demand, delivered energy and resource state.
  • At least one scripted but recoverable grid incident.
  • Functional maintenance/repair interaction.
  • Victory, failure, pause, restart and menu transitions.
  • Distinctive visual feedback, readable HUD and generated sound effects/music.
  • Offline operation with no external asset or network dependency for the prototype.
